Hackachusetts 2026

  • Hackathon
  • 2026

Boston’s hottest afternoons aren’t evenly hot: the neighborhoods with the least tree canopy run several degrees warmer than ones that can afford shade. Built at Hackachusetts 2026 with my teammate Ronaldo Fernandes, this planning tool is designed for city officials to track the current state of the heat island effect across all Boston neighborhoods, hear from residents on how they are feeling about the outdoor comfort, and utilize a simulator to see how actions such as planting trees, tree mix, cool roofs, and budget affect these temperatures.

How it works

The simulator is modelled on En-ROADS: controls along the bottom, consequences on top, updating as you drag. A planner sets a budget, picks a neighborhood, chooses a tree mix and a planting horizon, and watches the cooling curve move in response.

Showing the lag

Cooling is plotted year by year across thirty years of canopy growth, with a marker every four years for a mayoral term. A tree planted today cools a street decades from now, and the official approving it serves four years. That gap is the point of the tool rather than a footnote to it.

Pricing the alternative

Every scenario is matched against a cool-roof programme sized to deliver the same cooling. Paint is cheaper per degree and works this summer; trees take decades and bring shade, stormwater capture and air quality that paint does not. Both columns are shown, and the tool declines to pick between them.

Carrying what residents said

A feedback rail pins resident comments to the map — too hot, no shade, a bus stop with nowhere to stand, a park that is needed — so the numbers are not the only evidence in the room when a decision gets made.

Built for Sundai’s civic action track. The model is a handful of named constants and visible arithmetic, deliberately, so a planner challenged in a public meeting can say where each figure came from.
